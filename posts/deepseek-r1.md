DeepSeek-R1 drew considerable attention to the role of reinforcement learning in language-model reasoning. Its results highlighted a question extending beyond language modeling: how much useful reasoning behavior can emerge when a pretrained model is optimized through feedback on its outputs?

The January 2025 report is especially interesting alongside the reinforcement learning methods discussed in this blog. It applies policy optimization to language generation, showing how concepts developed for decision-making can transfer to a different domain.

## Main contributions

- **Reasoning-oriented reinforcement learning with GRPO.** Group Relative Policy Optimization estimates an advantage from a group of sampled responses, removing the separate value model typically used in PPO-style actor-critic training. GRPO was introduced in DeepSeekMath before being used in R1.
- **Reasoning transfer through distillation.** Responses generated by DeepSeek-R1 are used to train smaller models, including Qwen and Llama variants, yielding strong results with fewer model parameters.

The paper first presents DeepSeek-R1-Zero, trained with reinforcement learning without a preliminary supervised fine-tuning stage. It then addresses readability and mixed-language output through the additional stages of DeepSeek-R1. Finally, it examines transferring the resulting reasoning behavior to smaller models.

## Group Relative Policy Optimization

The R1-Zero experiment removes an initial supervised fine-tuning stage from the reasoning-training pipeline. This should not be confused with removing all prior data or training: the starting point is an already pretrained base language model.

It also differs from the claim that all reinforcement learning requires human preference labels. R1-Zero uses rule-based reward signals, including outcome correctness and response formatting, rather than a learned human-preference reward model for this stage.

### Removing the critic

PPO-style actor-critic training uses a value model to help estimate the advantage. At language-model scale, that critic can itself be a substantial model, with corresponding memory and training costs.

**GRPO replaces the learned value baseline with a baseline computed from a group of response rewards.**

<figure><img src="/assets/posts/71-3.png" alt="PPO estimates advantage using a value model, while GRPO compares rewards within a group of responses."><figcaption>PPO and GRPO, from the DeepSeekMath report.</figcaption></figure>

Instead of generating one response and evaluating it against a learned state value, sample G responses to the same prompt using the old policy. These responses form a group. Their relative scores supply the advantage estimates.

### Reading the objective

For prompt q, let the sampled responses be o₁ through oG. An outcome-level reward rᵢ is assigned to each response. The group-relative advantage is:

```math
\hat A_i=\frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(r_1,\ldots,r_G)}
{\operatorname{std}(r_1,\ldots,r_G)+\epsilon}.
```

The small epsilon prevents division by zero when the group has no reward variation. An above-average response receives positive advantage; a below-average response receives negative advantage.

The baseline is therefore obtained from the samples themselves, without a separate value model. **The update favors responses that perform better than the other responses to the same prompt.** It is a relative comparison, not a guarantee that the group's best response is correct.

For each generated token, define the current-to-old-policy probability ratio:

```math
\rho_{i,t}(\theta)=
\frac{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}.
```

With outcome supervision, the response's advantage is shared by its tokens. The objective averages a PPO-style clipped term and a KL penalty:

```math
\begin{aligned}
J_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[
\frac1G\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac1{|o_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|}
\Big(&\min\{\rho_{i,t}\hat A_i,
\operatorname{clip}(\rho_{i,t},1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\hat A_i\}\\
&-\beta\widehat D_{\mathrm{KL},i,t}\Big)\Bigg].
\end{aligned}
```

The ratio compares with the old policy that generated the data. The clipping range, controlled by epsilon, limits the incentive for large probability changes. The KL term regularizes the policy relative to a reference model.

The report uses the following token-level KL estimator. With the token and context understood, write u as the reference-to-current probability ratio:

```math
u=\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}
{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})},
\qquad \widehat D_{\mathrm{KL},i,t}=u-\log u-1.
```

The **old policy** and **reference policy** have different roles: one defines how the responses were sampled, while the other provides the regularization anchor. The main change from a critic-based advantage estimate is the group-normalized reward in front of the policy update.

Eliminating the value model saves its memory and optimization cost. It does not eliminate the cost of generating and evaluating multiple responses per prompt.

## Performance of DeepSeek-R1-Zero

The report describes reasoning-related behaviors emerging during reinforcement learning, including longer responses, checking, and reconsidering a solution.

<figure><img src="/assets/posts/71-7.png" alt="Average response length grows during DeepSeek-R1-Zero training."><figcaption>Response length during R1-Zero training, from the original report.</figcaption></figure>

As training progresses, average response length increases. This indicates that the model allocates more generated tokens to its response, consistent with longer reasoning attempts. The interesting observation is that such behavior develops under reward optimization without a preceding set of supervised reasoning demonstrations in this stage.

Length alone does not prove that a chain of reasoning is correct or faithful. The behavioral observation should be considered together with task performance, rather than treating more tokens as sufficient evidence of better reasoning.

## DeepSeek-R1

R1-Zero has readability and language-mixing problems. DeepSeek-R1 adds a sequence of training stages: cold-start examples with a readable format, reasoning-oriented RL with correctness and language-consistency rewards, expanded supervised fine-tuning, and a final broader RL stage.

### 1. Cold start: supervised fine-tuning bootstrap

The base model is first fine-tuned on a small, high-quality set of long chain-of-thought demonstrations. This avoids some of the unstable early behavior of pure reasoning RL and makes outputs more readable.

The cold-start examples are collected through prompting strategies such as few-shot long-form reasoning and reflection or verification prompts, as well as by reformatting R1-Zero outputs. Human post-processing helps refine the examples.

Filtering enforces a consistent, readable format with an explicit final summary. The reasoning and summary are separated so that a long reasoning trajectory still leads to an accessible final answer.

### 2. Reasoning-oriented RL and language consistency

Starting from the cold-start model, large-scale reinforcement learning focuses on reasoning-intensive tasks such as mathematics, code, science, and logic where correctness can be evaluated.

Because RL can produce mixed-language reasoning, a language-consistency reward measures the proportion of target-language words in the reasoning output. It is added to the task-accuracy reward.

This introduces a deliberate tradeoff: readability and consistent language are prioritized even when the added constraint slightly reduces peak task performance. Correctness is not the only property of a useful response.

### 3. Rejection sampling and expanded supervised fine-tuning

After reasoning RL converges, the resulting checkpoint generates a much larger dataset for the next supervised stage.

For reasoning prompts, multiple candidate solutions are sampled and correct responses are retained through rejection sampling. Some tasks use model-based judging rather than relying only on a simple rule-based check. Unreadable trajectories, including language mixing and poorly formatted long outputs, are filtered. The resulting reasoning data contains approximately 600,000 examples.

Approximately 200,000 non-reasoning examples are added, covering writing, factual question answering, translation, and self-description. Much of this data follows the DeepSeek-V3 pipeline or reuses earlier supervised data.

The base model is then fine-tuned for two epochs on the combined dataset of approximately 800,000 examples. This stage broadens the model beyond the narrower reasoning tasks used during the preceding RL phase.

### 4. Reinforcement learning for broader scenarios

A final reinforcement learning stage improves helpfulness and harmlessness across general user tasks while retaining reasoning performance.

Reasoning prompts continue to use rule-based rewards. General prompts use reward models reflecting human preferences. Helpfulness is judged mainly from the final summary so that the evaluation does not unnecessarily interfere with the underlying reasoning process.

Harmlessness is evaluated over the entire output, including reasoning and summary, so that problematic content is not overlooked merely because the final answer is acceptable.

The full R1 pipeline therefore combines several forms of supervision. The “without preliminary SFT” setting describes R1-Zero, not every stage of R1.

## Distillation

The paper also transfers reasoning behavior to smaller models by fine-tuning them on generated responses.

<figure><img src="/assets/posts/71-8.png" alt="Table 5 compares reasoning benchmark results for distilled DeepSeek-R1 models and other models."><figcaption>Distillation results from the original DeepSeek-R1 report. Comparisons apply to the listed metrics and evaluation settings.</figcaption></figure>

The table shows that relatively small distilled models can outperform some larger comparison models on the reported reasoning benchmarks. This illustrates the potential of transferring strong reasoning examples rather than relying solely on additional scale.

Here distillation is primarily supervised learning from teacher-generated responses, not necessarily matching the teacher's full probability distribution. The paper also identifies applying reinforcement learning to distilled models as a direction that may improve them further.

The result is benchmark-specific. It should not be expanded into a claim that every distilled model is better than every non-reasoning model across all tasks.

## Conclusion

The work highlights reinforcement learning's relevance beyond traditional games and control tasks. Policy optimization, reward construction, and relative action evaluation become tools for improving language-model reasoning.

Its significance lies in the combination: R1-Zero studies reasoning behavior induced by reinforcement learning, R1 adds the data and alignment stages needed for more usable outputs, and distillation transfers some of the resulting capability into smaller models. The connection between these stages illustrates how ideas from different areas of machine learning can reinforce one another.
